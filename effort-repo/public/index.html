      01 / WHY WEATHER IS A TRAINING VARIABLE

      Running is a heat engine with poor efficiency. Roughly three-quarters of the metabolic energy you produce leaves as heat rather than forward motion, and at marathon effort that is on the order of a kilowatt of thermal load your body has to shed continuously to keep core temperature stable. Almost all of it goes through the evaporation of sweat. This is the fact the rest of the app is built on: your ability to hold a pace is limited less by how hot the air is than by how much of your heat that air will accept.

      Which is why dew point matters more than temperature. Dew point is the vapour pressure of the air, and evaporation is driven by the gradient between saturated skin — about 5.6 kPa at 35 °C — and the air around you. As dew point climbs, that gradient narrows and your primary cooling channel closes, whatever the thermometer says. It also explains why heat and humidity compound rather than add: heat inflates the numerator of the heat-balance equation while humidity shrinks the denominator. A second ceiling sits underneath both. Even in perfectly dry air you can only sweat so fast, which is why 95 °F in the desert still costs a marathoner close to five percent of their pace — a case that a pure humidity model gets badly wrong.

      The consequences are measurable and large. Across 3,891 marathon performances at 754 races, the optimal air temperature for the event sits near 48 °F, humidity is essentially irrelevant below about 65 °F, and genuinely hostile conditions cost upward of eight percent — fifteen minutes on a three-hour marathon. But the single biggest source of variation is not the weather at all. It is you. Cyclists tested in the heat lost sixteen percent of their power output unacclimatised, eight percent after one week of exposure, and three percent after two: a fivefold swing from identical conditions, driven entirely by what the athlete had been training in beforehand. EffortCast reads that history from your own last fortnight of weather and prices the day accordingly, which is the difference between a forecast and a training decision.
